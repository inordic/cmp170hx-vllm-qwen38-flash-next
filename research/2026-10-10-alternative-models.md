# Keep Flash-Next, upgrade its quant instead

As of October 2026, no open-weight model gives this box both better agentic-coding quality and faster prefill than Swift 1.5 IQ3_XXS on Strata, and every realistic upgrade stays inside the Qwen3.8-Flash-Next family. Only five 2026 releases fit in 128 GB of VRAM, and only one of them, **GLM-5.3-Flash, beats Flash-Next on the one independent yardstick, the Artificial Analysis (AA) Intelligence Index, by 42 to 40**. On this hardware GLM has to run at about 3 bits per weight, where its KL divergence is about 1.7x Flash-Next's at the same quant tier, and its **measured cold prefill on an identical 2x CMP 170HX pair is about 1.5K tok/s, so a 32K prompt takes 21–28 s instead of today's 7 s**. DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731, MiMo-V2.6-Flash, Qwen3.8-27B, Gemma 4 31B and Nemotron 3.5 Lightning all score lower on independent tests and have no fast two-card SM80 path. The best-supported move is **GSQ-RCO IQ3_S on Strata**, a drop-in that roughly halves KL divergence for about 6.5 GiB more VRAM and an estimated 5–10% prefill cost. The only option with published evidence of beating today's prefill and today's quality at once is **AWQ W4A16 with an FP8 n-gram table on vllm-backport**, which needs the 96 GB RAM upgrade and whose best numbers (4.7K tok/s at 4K, 5.4K at 32K) came from a privately patched build. Separately, UkisAI's own data show Swift giving up 5.9 DeepSWE points, and 2.6 GPQA points at the medium effort this box uses, which makes a Swift-versus-base comparison the cheapest quality experiment available. Treat every quality delta below as provisional: nearly all agentic benchmarks are vendor-run, and no study measures 3-bit against 4-bit on agentic tasks for any of these models.

## Five models fit in 128 GB, and only GLM-5.3-Flash outscores Flash-Next

Most of 2026's open-weight frontier cannot run in 128 GB of VRAM at all. Kimi K3 (2.8T total), Qwen3.8-2.4T-A95B, MiMo-V2.6-Pro (1T), DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash (a 552B backbone plus a 196B Engram table, 763B in safetensors), the full 753B GLM-5.3 and Mistral Large 4 (about 1.05T, weights promised for late October) have no realistic configuration on this box ([Wikipedia on Kimi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kimi_(AI)); [Codersera](https://codersera.com/blog/qwen-3-8-model-lineup-2026/); [eWeek](https://www.eweek.com/news/xiaomi-mimo-v26-open-source-rl-reproduction/); [DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash card](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash); [AA QFN vs GLM-5.3](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/qwen3-8-flash-next-vs-glm-5-3); [MarkTechPost](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/06/mistral-ai-releases-mistral-large-4-le-chonk-a-1-05t-parameter-open-weight-multimodal-moe/)). **MiniMax M3 (428B/23B) fits only as a 1-bit build whose 128 GB file leaves no room for KV cache** ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/minimax-m3)). Qwen has released neither a Qwen3.8 35B-A3B nor a 122B-class model, so Flash-Next has no mid-size sibling ([Codersera](https://codersera.com/blog/qwen-3-8-model-lineup-2026/)), and the only "Nemotron 3.5 Super" that has surfaced is a midtrain checkpoint rather than an instruct release ([NeMo docs](https://docs.nvidia.com/nemo/automodel/model-coverage/omni/nvidia/nemotron-3-5-super-vl)). That leaves three 280–320B sparse MoEs, two dense ~30B models and one small MoE.

Comparing their quality is harder than the vendor tables suggest. Each lab reports its own mix of DeepSWE 1.1, Terminal-Bench 2.1, Toolathlon-Verified and SWE-bench Pro on its own harness, and none of the August–September releases has SWE-bench Verified, Aider Polyglot or BFCL numbers; DataCamp declined to name a GLM-versus-Flash-Next winner for exactly this reason ([DataCamp](https://www.datacamp.com/blog/glm-5-3-flash-vs-qwen3-8-flash-next)). Independent re-runs, where they exist, come in lower: DeepSeek's **Terminal-Bench 2.1 score of 82.7 on its own unreleased harness became 78.7 in AA's run** ([DeepSeek card](https://huggingface.co/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731); [OpenRouter](https://openrouter.ai/deepseek/deepseek-v4-flash-0731)). On AA's harder **Terminal-Bench 4.0, Flash-Next scores 25%, DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 12% and Qwen3.8-27B 6%**, which makes Flash-Next the strongest terminal agent in the group that has a score ([AA QFN vs DeepSeek](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/qwen3-8-flash-next-vs-deepseek-v4-flash); [AA QFN vs 27B](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/qwen3-8-flash-next-vs-qwen3-8-27b)). AA's index itself needs care: it was rebased from v4.1.1 to v4.3.2, moving Flash-Next from 56 to 40 and GLM from 57 to 42, and that rebase is confirmed only by secondary sources ([Codersera](https://codersera.com/blog/qwen-3-8-flash-complete-guide-2026/)). A two-point gap is small enough that harness or sampling noise could account for it.

## A 3 GB/s link pins every 300B challenger at 3 bits and slow prefill

The box's hardware settles most of the question before quality comes into it. Gen2 x8 measures **3.1–3.2 GB/s per direction, about 7.8x slower than an RTX 3090 on Gen4 x16**, and anything that streams expert weights from host RAM on every token is "off the table" on such a link ([magicsmokepress](https://github.com/magicsmokepress/cmp-170hx-vllm)). Tensor parallelism is also out: vLLM TP2 on two CMPs without P2P prefilled at about 2,500 tok/s, against 4.5–5.0K with forced P2P ([sekkit](https://gist.github.com/sekkit/e83c16d1eeeb1e16e08d67b28901a66c)), so any model above 64 GB has to run as a pipeline or layer split. Flash-Next's n-gram table is the exception to the offload rule, because it is a sparse row lookup that works even from SSD ([magicsmokepress](https://github.com/magicsmokepress/cmp-170hx-vllm)). That asymmetry has a consequence for the RAM upgrade that is easy to miss: **96 GB of RAM lets Flash-Next move its n-gram table off the GPUs, but it does not let GLM, DeepSeek or MiMo run their 4-bit-class builds (about 155–200 GB)**, because their offloaded experts would cross a 3 GB/s link on every token.

GLM-5.3-Flash is the only challenger with an independent quality lead, and the only one measured on identical hardware, in the stack you already run. EXL3 3.05 bpw keeps 116.6 GiB of weights resident in a layer split without P2P on ExLlamaV3 1.5.4, with about 4.5 GiB of Q8 KV cache at 384K context ([Flun](https://github.com/Flun/glm53-flash-cmp170hx-exl3)). It measures **1,529 tok/s prefill and 95.6 tok/s decode with the DFlash2 drafter, still about 90 tok/s decode at a 385K input** ([llamaperf](https://llamaperf.com/gpu/cmp-170hx-64gb)). A 32K follow-up took 28.5 s with full reprocessing and 1.1 s with prefix reuse ([Flun](https://github.com/Flun/glm53-flash-cmp170hx-exl3)). The slow prefill belongs to the two-card EXL3 path rather than the model: on four CMPs, the W4A16 vLLM forks reach **5.6–7.4K tok/s cold prefill** ([Morrowmake](https://github.com/Morrowmake/glm53-flash-cmp170hx-recipe/blob/main/docs/results.md); [liumorrisclaw](https://github.com/liumorrisclaw/cmp170hx-glm53-lab)).

GLM also loses more than Flash-Next does when squeezed to 3 bits. Unsloth's **UD-IQ3_XXS keeps 81.6% top-1 agreement at KLD 0.284 for GLM, against 85.4% and 0.165 for Flash-Next at the same tier** ([Unsloth GLM](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/glm-5.3-flash); [Unsloth QFN](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3.8-next)). A selective EXL3 3.0 bpw build that keeps attention in BF16 still showed KL 0.153 and **+9.3% perplexity**, failing both of its author's own quality gates ([0xSero](https://huggingface.co/0xSero/GLM-5.3-Flash-EXL3-3.0bpw)). In GLM's favour, it used **180M output tokens to run the AA index against Flash-Next's 245M** ([AA](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/glm-5-3-flash)), and an anecdotal eight-task head-to-head scored it 63/80 against 56/80, with both models perfect on the agentic-pipeline task ([daily.dev](https://daily.dev/posts/qwen-3-8-flash-next-fully-tested-vs-glm-5-3-flash-you-can-run-it-locally-it-s-crazy--ozyxaixvw)). Against it, the DFlash2 drafter that makes its decode fast carries a non-commercial **CC-BY-NC-ND-4.0 licence** ([SGLang cookbook](https://lmsysorg.mintlify.app/cookbook/autoregressive/GLM/GLM-5.3-Flash)), and its prefix-reuse path did not fully reproduce full-reprocess outputs at 8K and 32K ([Flun](https://github.com/Flun/glm53-flash-cmp170hx-exl3)).

DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 and MiMo-V2.6-Flash lose on independent quality and have no measured fast two-card path. DeepSeek is the most token-frugal model here, at **62k output tokens per AA task against Flash-Next's 108k** ([AA](https://artificialanalysis.ai/models/comparisons/qwen3-8-flash-next-vs-deepseek-v4-flash)), but its index score is six points lower. Its only fast SM80 path, native FP4 on vLLM at about 155 GiB, was measured on four CMPs at 5.3K prefill and 98 tok/s decode, while on four CMPs llama.cpp managed only 450 tok/s prefill and 29 tok/s decode with a Q4 build ([llamaperf](https://llamaperf.com/gpu/cmp-170hx-64gb)). What fits two cards is the 103 GB llama.cpp IQ3_XXS plus about 10 GB for the DSpark drafter, a route with no SM80 measurement at all ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/deepseek-v4)). MiMo's vendor DeepSWE of 67.9 is the highest in the group, but it is unconfirmed and AA ranks the model below both GLM and Flash-Next. At the 2.27 bpw that fits, one EXL3 build scored **50.0% on GPQA-Diamond against 56.2% for the FP8 reference** ([benthecarman](https://huggingface.co/benthecarman/MiMo-V2.6-Flash-RL-exl3)), and llama.cpp's MiMo MTP sidecar currently fails to load ([llama.cpp #29345](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/issues/29345)). Scaling from GLM's measured run suggests MiMo would prefill at about 1.5–2K tok/s on two cards, an estimate rather than a measurement.

The dense and small models lose on both axes. Qwen3.8-27B on the Reddit poster's CMP box (quant and GPU count unrecorded) prefilled at 1.8–2.1K tok/s at short context and about 1.1K at 150K, with decode falling from 119–242 tok/s to 65–82 at 252K ([Flun bench](https://github.com/Flun/ai-workstation-orchestrator/blob/HEAD/llm_bench.json)), and its Terminal-Bench 4.0 score is a quarter of Flash-Next's. Gemma 4 31B on the same box fell from 1.7K to 640 tok/s prefill by 64K, with decode collapsing to 51 tok/s or lower ([Flun bench](https://github.com/Flun/ai-workstation-orchestrator/blob/HEAD/llm_bench.json)). Nemotron 3.5 Lightning's Terminal-Bench 2.1 score of 24.6 rules it out for agentic coding ([NVIDIA](https://huggingface.co/nvidia/NVIDIA-Nemotron-3.5-Lightning-30B-A3B-NVFP4)).

## Swift's shorter thinking costs points on agentic coding

Swift 1.5 trades accuracy for shorter thinking, and its losses land on the benchmarks closest to this box's workload. UkisAI's own BF16 comparison at xhigh effort shows the biggest drop on DeepSWE, the benchmark that most resembles "Claude Code on a repo" ([UkisAI card](https://huggingface.co/ukisai/Swift1.5-Qwen3.8-Flash-Next)):

| Benchmark | Base Flash-Next | Swift 1.5 | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| DeepSWE 1.1 | 54.87% | 48.97% | **−5.9** |
| IFBench | 73.20 | 70.13 | −3.1 |
| Terminal-Bench 2.1 | 67.64% | 69.66% | +2.0 |
| LiveCodeBench v6 | 88.40% | 90.39% | +2.0 |

On Terminal-Bench 2.1, Swift's mean output tokens actually rose 11.9%. The effort level matters as well: at medium effort, this box's default, **GPQA-Diamond falls from 86.36 to 83.74**, against a loss of only 0.2 points at xhigh ([Swift GGUF card](https://huggingface.co/ukisai/Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-GGUF)). The token saving is real but smaller than claimed. HelixML's independent pilot measured **28.8% fewer thinking tokens and 1.40x faster solutions at equal accuracy, with identical decode speed**, though its author calls it a two-question pilot and did not reproduce UkisAI's 1.8x claim ([HelixML](https://helix.ml/blog/swift-flash-next-four-gpu-evaluation)).

Switching back to the base model has a measurable cost. At medium effort, base Flash-Next averaged 4,157 tokens per GPQA answer against Swift's 2,483, which at about 110 tok/s works out to roughly 15 s more per reasoning turn. Prefill does not change, because Swift and base share the same architecture, so the comparison costs one download and targets the largest documented quality loss in the current setup.

The tool-calling failures reported for Flash-Next point somewhere other than the weights. An SGLang token-0 loop appears only with thinking mode, tools and the `qwen3_coder` parser all active ([sglang #36537](https://github.com/sgl-project/sglang/issues/36537)), and users report empty turns after tool results and invented user tasks ([HF discussions](https://huggingface.co/Qwen/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next/discussions)). These cluster around the chat template and tool parser and apply to Swift and base alike. My inference is that **the template and parser layer moves agentic reliability as much as the choice between Swift and base**, so your custom chromix/froggerick template deserves the same scrutiny as the weights.

## Inside Flash-Next, one quant tier and one engine swap are the real levers

Today's quant is better than its name suggests. ISTA's GSQ-RCO IQ3_XXS keeps **99.4% of BF16's task average (92.57 against 93.12 across AIME25, GPQA-D and LCB v6)** ([ISTA-DASLab](https://huggingface.co/ISTA-DASLab/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-GSQ-RCO-GGUF)), and Kaitchup found every standard Flash-Next GGUF down to 1-bit cleared a 95% accuracy-recovery threshold ([Kaitchup](https://kaitchup.substack.com/p/qwen38-flash-next-gguf-benchmark)). Those tests are all single-turn, however, and UkisAI itself recommends **Q6_K or higher for long agentic runs with strict tool-call formatting** ([Swift GGUF card](https://huggingface.co/ukisai/Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-GGUF)). Q6_K is 168 GB and cannot be fully resident here, so the practical ceiling is about 4-bit.

### IQ3_S halves KL divergence for 6.5 GiB more VRAM

IQ3_S is the clearest step up. In Swift's held-out tests, moving from IQ3_XXS to IQ3_S cuts KL divergence roughly in half ([KLD TSV](https://huggingface.co/ukisai/Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-GSQ-RCO-GGUF/blob/main/evaluation/heldout-kld.tsv); [Swift GSQ-RCO card](https://huggingface.co/ukisai/Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-GSQ-RCO-GGUF)), and ISTA's IQ3_S reaches a task average of **93.26, at or above BF16**:

| Test set | IQ3_XXS KLD | IQ3_S KLD |
|---|---|---|
| Prose | 0.116 | 0.063 |
| Code | 0.119 | 0.069 |
| Development set | 0.240 | 0.145 |

Strata's fused int8 prompt kernels cover IQ3_S gate/up and IQ4_NL down projections, so the whole pack stays on the fast path, and Strata's own per-layer timings put IQ3_S about 6–10% slower than IQ3_XXS ([moe_fused_iq.cu](https://github.com/Niko1221/Strata/blob/main/src/prefill/moe_fused_iq.cu)). Experts grow from 43 to 50 GB while the 26.8 GiB n-gram table stays the same, which means about 73 GiB on the GPUs instead of 67 and no extra RAM ([Strata MODELS.md](https://github.com/Niko1221/Strata/blob/main/docs/MODELS.md)). That document still says Swift has no IQ3_S even though UkisAI now publishes an 83.74 GB one, so expect to build the pack and config by hand.

The bigger quants are not clean improvements on this box. **UD-IQ4_XS** sits in roughly the same quality class as IQ3_S but costs more: Strata documents a 48 GB RAM minimum for it, a layer split loads all its experts (about 55 GiB) into RAM, its `--compat-bf16` pack raised perplexity 6–9% on AMD, and five of its down-projection layers fall outside the fused kernels ([UNSLOTH_Q4.md](https://github.com/Niko1221/Strata/blob/main/docs/UNSLOTH_Q4.md)). **UD-Q4_K_XL** is a genuine quality step, at KLD 0.047 in Unsloth's table and 0.0084 on turboderp's trace, but stock Strata has no fused CUDA kernel for its K-quants: it dequantizes them to FP16 at about 4x the GPU time and loads all 77 GB of experts into RAM at start on a split ([UNSLOTH_Q4.md](https://github.com/Niko1221/Strata/blob/main/docs/UNSLOTH_Q4.md)). **EXL3 4.05 bpw** has the best fidelity that fits, at **KLD 0.0067** in about 107 GB including a 6-bit n-gram table ([turboderp](https://huggingface.co/turboderp/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-exl3); [doth4580](https://huggingface.co/doth4580/Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-EXL3-4.05bpw)). Every EXL3 MoE data point near this hardware prefills well below Strata, though: a 3.05 bpw Flash-Next build on a 3090 Ti plus a 3090, with 20% CPU offload, ran at 1,841 tok/s at 4K ([ExLlamaV3 v1.5.0](https://github.com/turboderp-org/exllamav3/releases/tag/v1.5.0)), and GLM runs at 1.53K tok/s on this very box.

### vllm-backport is the only route with higher published prefill

The engine swap is the only route with published evidence of higher prefill on this hardware class. wtdcode's vllm-backport supports Flash-Next AWQ on an SM80 image and keeps the roughly 51 GiB FP8 n-gram table in pinned host RAM ([vllm-backport](https://github.com/wtdcode/vllm-backport)). The Reddit poster's script runs it as PP2 with a 24/24 layer split, MTP-3 and private environment flags ([run script](https://github.com/Flun/ai-workstation-orchestrator/blob/HEAD/run_swift15_qwen38_fp8ple.sh)), and the poster's bench file shows **2,571 tok/s prefill at 1K, 4,663 at 4K, 5,367 at 32K and 4,733 at 150K, with 105–145 tok/s decode** ([llm_bench.json](https://github.com/Flun/ai-workstation-orchestrator/blob/HEAD/llm_bench.json)). Those figures need caution. The file does not record which GPUs ran the test, earlier runs plateaued at 3.4–3.5K, the public README's best figure is 3,457 tok/s ([Flun README](https://github.com/Flun/ai-workstation-orchestrator)), and another 2x CMP W4A16 report measured 3.2–3.3K prefill and 70.5 s TTFT on a 200K prompt ([llamaperf](https://llamaperf.com/gpu/cmp-170hx-64gb)). INT4 group-128 experts with BF16 dense weights put this build in the 4-bit class, but no KLD has been published for it. By analogy with NVFP4 W4A16 on turboderp's trace (0.0100, against 0.0349 for UD-IQ3_XXS), its KL divergence is probably 2–3x lower than today's, an inference rather than a measurement.

**Option 1** is the only upgrade you can run this week without the RAM upgrade, and its quality gain is the best documented of anything here: halved KL divergence and BF16 parity on ISTA's three-benchmark average. Its 5–10% prefill penalty is an estimate from per-layer kernel timings, not a measurement on SM80, so benchmark it at 4K, 32K and 128K before switching production. Run it as two arms: Swift IQ3_S keeps today's token budget, while ISTA's base IQ3_S tests whether recovering Swift's DeepSWE and IFBench losses is worth about 1.7x the thinking tokens at medium effort. Swift's tiers share no files, so moving to Swift IQ3_S is a full 83.7 GB download ([HF API](https://huggingface.co/api/models/ukisai/Swift-1.5-Qwen3.8-Flash-Next-GSQ-RCO-GGUF/tree/main)).

**Option 2** has the most upside, as the only candidate with published evidence of beating today's setup on both prefill and quant fidelity, but its headline numbers depend on unpublished patches, and the reproducible public figures sit 20–35% below Strata at long context. Several known issues need handling. PP2 with MTP and prefix caching produced a silent constant-token loop on GLM with three or more concurrent requests, and its root cause sits in hybrid-model spec-decode block tables that Flash-Next's linear-attention layers plausibly share ([#96](https://github.com/wtdcode/vllm-backport/issues/96)). The gloo control plane needs `GLOO_SOCKET_IFNAME=lo`, a fix that gave +53% decode at 512K on CMPs ([#111](https://github.com/wtdcode/vllm-backport/issues/111)) and that LXC networking will probably need too (inference). Plain PP with the offloaded n-gram table needed a patch ([#36](https://github.com/wtdcode/vllm-backport/issues/36)), and router logits rounding through BF16 on CC 8.0 change expert selection unless a backported fix is applied ([#65](https://github.com/wtdcode/vllm-backport/issues/65)). Pinning roughly 50 GiB inside a Proxmox LXC also needs the container's memlock limit raised to match (inference). The deciding measurement is follow-up TTFT inside a real agent loop rather than the cold-prefill table: most agentic turns hit the prefix cache, so Strata's 0.2 s follow-up is the bar to clear.

**Option 3** is a quality probe, not a replacement. It fails the prefill requirement outright, so it earns a place only if it solves tasks that options 1 or 2 cannot. If it does, GLM still has a role on this box as a hard-problem model, used where cold TTFT matters less and its ~27% lower token use pays for itself.

Several candidates did not make the list. DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 and MiMo-V2.6-Flash score lower on independent tests and have no measured two-card path, Qwen3.8-27B and Gemma 4 31B slow down sharply at long context, and an EXL3 4.05 bpw Flash-Next build is worth a single run only as a near-lossless reference for how much quantization matters on your own tasks. Two releases are worth watching: **StepFun's Step 5 Preview**, with open weights promised for 15 October at an unknown size ([eesel](https://www.eesel.ai/blog/stepfun-step-5)), and **Ornith-1.5-35B-A3B**, which claims SWE-bench Verified 79 and Terminal-Bench 2.1 67.8 at 3B active but has no third-party reproduction ([Ornith card](https://huggingface.co/ornith-ai/Ornith-1.5-35B-A3B); [OrcaRouter](https://www.orcarouter.ai/blog/ornith-1-5-open-weights-explained)). To rank the shortlisted options, run the same 20–30 repo tasks on each at medium effort, recording pass rate, total output tokens, wall-clock time per task, and cold and follow-up TTFT at 10K, 32K and 128K.

## Conclusion

The binding constraint on this box is the 128 GB, 3 GB/s envelope, not which models exist. It forces every 300B-class rival down to about 3 bits on a slower engine, while Flash-Next's 6B active parameters and offloadable n-gram table fit the hardware almost exactly, so **the 96 GB RAM upgrade widens Flash-Next's lead instead of opening the door to rivals**. The picture changes only with more cards: on four CMPs over Gen2 x4 links without P2P, GLM-5.3-Flash W4A16 in PP4 prefilled a 482K prompt at 5.6–5.8K tok/s and decoded code at 167 tok/s ([liumorrisclaw](https://github.com/liumorrisclaw/cmp170hx-glm53-lab)). If a stronger model is the goal, the realistic path is two more cards on a platform that can host them, not a different model on two.

Until then, quality on this box depends more on quant fidelity, the Swift trade-off and the serving template than on model choice. The public evidence is too thin to settle those questions, with vendor-run agentic scores, no 3-bit versus 4-bit agentic study and no SM80 Strata numbers for any quant above IQ3_XXS, so a one-day A/B on your own agent tasks will tell you more than any leaderboard cited here.
